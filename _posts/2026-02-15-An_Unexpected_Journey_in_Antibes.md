Antibes was never in the plan. This was supposed to be a coastal trip heading east from Arles,[^1] starting in Cannes, threading through Nice, Monaco,[^2] Ventimiglia, spanning the entire Côte d'Azur.[^3] SNCF's[^4] regional pricing brought the fare down to a minimum: thirty-five euros for three days, fifty for seven. If you were willing to practice a few rusty phrases of French with the elegant ladies past fifty at the platform ticket counter, you could save the two-euro card fee from the self-service machine — a broke exchange student's indignity doesn't end at the boarding pass paired with economy class in the terminal;[^5] you must also pinch every penny like the Dutch and the Vlaamse,[^6] or when nature calls in France, that cash-only pay toilet becomes your true reckoning.

[^1]: Arles — an ancient city in southern Provence, France, on the banks of the Rhône, famous for Van Gogh's residence and work there.
[^2]: Monaco — a microstate in southwestern Europe, surrounded on three sides by France, bordering the Mediterranean to the south. The world's second-smallest country (after the Vatican), known for extreme wealth.
[^3]: Côte d'Azur — the Mediterranean coast of southern France, renowned for sun, beaches, and the resort life.
[^4]: SNCF — *Société Nationale des Chemins de fer Français*, the French National Railway Company.
[^5]: Terminal refers to the airport departure hall; intra-EU flights are almost exclusively narrow-body, direct, and all-economy.
[^6]: Vlaamse refers to the Dutch-speaking region of Belgium; Dutch-speakers are stereotypically tight with money, as evidenced by the phrase "going Dutch."

Antibes entered the plan because my attitude toward travel has always been cautious. Before setting out, while drafting the itinerary, I compared prices carefully, from Charolais steak[^7] to Airbnb[^8] rentals; I collected my French friends' experiences and noted the one-star reviews on Trip.com. Cannes's prices finally told me everything. I knew the Palme d'Or was awarded here, knew those directors and stars walked the red carpet toward eternity or oblivion, knew the name Théâtre Claude Debussy[^9] already carried a price tag. But the festival's glow has nothing to do with tourists, just as fireworks have nothing to do with the street sweeper. Cannes in May belongs to the stars; the rest of the year belongs to the landlords. I had fantasized about lying in a Cannes King's Bed watching the coastline shimmer in the distance, but the Canopy[^10] price tag had its own answer: you leave, the dream stays.

[^7]: Charolais steak — Charolais cattle originate from Burgundy in east-central France, pasture-raised year-round. Unlike the richly marbled Japanese Wagyu familiar to most Chinese diners, Charolais is lean with very little fat, yet extraordinarily tender, carrying a deep, robust "beefy" flavor — a prestige ingredient in fine French restaurants.
[^8]: Airbnb — a short-term rental platform where hosts rent rooms or entire homes to travelers.
[^9]: Théâtre Claude Debussy — the main venue of the Cannes Film Festival, where the red carpet and premieres take place.
[^10]: Canopy — the Canopy by Hilton hotel brand, luxury four-star hotel. 

So I stopped fantasizing and chose Antibes. Beyond its three one-Michelin-star restaurants,[^11] lodging options were ample. My budget swelled alongside U.S. equities.[^12] Abandoning my habit of sorting by price ascending, I found a charming and cheap place on Airbnb's Guest-favorite list; for the first time booking in France, someone saw my East Asian face and replied in mine. I paid for that bargain soon enough. Lime e-bike[^13] could only park at the foot of the hill; the apartment, like a mansion in Beverly Hills,[^14] sat five hundred meters away and about fifty meters up on the Av. de la Rostagne. My French host, nearing sixty and about to retire, heard I had a lab meeting[^15] starting soon. He came up smiling, helped me wrestle the Wi-Fi into acceptable latency, and set out hand-ground coffee. His English carried the same awkwardness as my four months of French, but that didn't stop him from asking eagerly whether that Michelin lunch was to my taste.

[^11]: Michelin star — a restaurant rating system; "one star" typically means "worth a visit," up to a maximum of three.
[^12]: U.S. equities — the American stock market.
[^13]: Lime App; in French, lime is commonly called *citron vert* ("green lemon").
[^14]: Beverly Hills — the affluent enclave in Los Angeles, shorthand for "where the mansions cluster."
[^15]: Lab meeting — common academic jargon for a research group's regular internal discussion.

*"You're quite well-informed, you know. During the Palme d'Or festival, they don't stay in Cannes — they stay at my place."*

This was a ground-floor apartment[^16] hidden on the hillside above the coast, wrapped by an enormous terrace. No sea view, but tropical plants everywhere; when the wind rose, you could smell the Mediterranean. Its refinements didn't end at the terrace, which seemed longer and more winding than my own path in life. My wallet, squeezed by European labor costs, drove me to cook for myself on every trip to France — or at least heat up American-style microwave cuisine. But this kitchen, larger than my loft[^17] living room, had every pot and utensil imaginable, four grades of salt, not to mention bottles and jars of Eastern spices. I thought of that coffee shop in Hangzhou: a three-group La Marzocco[^18] boiler steaming its mist onto the mugs overhead, sunlight refracting spectra onto their surfaces — world landmarks and scripts in every language, narrating backpackers' travels across the globe through wisps of steam. Here, fridge magnets covered all three doors. At the junction between upper and lower compartments: the Golden Gate Bridge.[^19] That place I once ached for, on the other side of the earth, was already behind me. I planted myself in front of the fridge for a while. 

[^16]: In Europe, floors are counted from zero; the ground floor corresponds to the first floor in China.
[^17]: Loft — an open, high-ceilinged living space.
[^18]: La Marzocco — an Italian high-end commercial espresso machine brand, common in specialty cafés. "Three-group" refers to a top-tier commercial model with three brew heads.
[^19]: Golden Gate Bridge — the iconic suspension bridge in San Francisco.

Stunned by the Côte d'Azur × the Alps, I decided to launch a forgotten Tour de France[^20] of my own. Limited by time and budget, I could only purchase a three-hundred-minute LimePass. At eight, just as the sky brightened, I slung my camera over my shoulder and rode downhill. At first the path was narrow, jolting over tram tracks. Another half kilometer and I reached the coast — suddenly, the world opened wide. Antibes's coastline is split by a spike of a peninsula: to the west, Juan-les-Pins[^21] looks out at the bustle of distant Cannes; to the east, the Baie des Anges[^22] is the Antibes that visitors come to see. My lodging was only five hundred meters from Juan-les-Pins, so naturally I followed the pattern of this trip — head east, riding the peninsula's hills deeper into the Mediterranean.

[^20]: Tour de France — the French bicycle race; used here to mean "traveling by bike through the land, Tour-de-France style."
[^21]: Juan-les-Pins — a seaside town on the Côte d'Azur, administratively part of Antibes.
[^22]: Baie des Anges — French for "Bay of Angels," the bay near Nice.

I wasn't the only early riser. An Englishman with a Manchester accent jogged with his dog; now and then, road cyclists — solo or in clusters — screamed past on the opposite lane. As luck would have it, the coastal ride ended at the entrance of some luxury hotel: a construction vehicle blocking the road with a terse French sign informed everyone that the way was closed. Every alternate route climbed. I had neither the stamina to scale mountains, nor thousands of euros for a carbon-fiber bicycle, nor insurance good enough to summon a helicopter if something went wrong. The green Lime e-bike, conspicuously out of place, was already the optimal solution. Still, climbing, I had to grumble: capped at twenty-five km/h, the motor was simply not enough.

The itinerary was grand. The nobleman Liu Ziji of Nanyang never found the Peach Blossom Spring,[^23] yet I found Antibes without searching. The Alps unfolded at a turn. Nearby, you didn't need to swim out to see the sea's deep blue, mirroring cerulean sky and white clouds under the high-pressure sun. In the distance, that had to be Nice: the airport awaiting a four-engine giant[^24], its roar cleaving the sky ahead of the enormous wake turbulence, announcing its arrival. On the other side of the airport, travelers were flying toward the future. My mind drifted back to Nyingchi in Tibet: a thirteen-year-old boy gazing down at the Yarlung Tsangpo River, looking up at the golden triangle of Namcha Barwa. Before reaching Cairo or the British Museum, this was my pyramid. I held up my phone, the Alps and the Mediterranean shared the frame. A plane inched across the Live Photo. I thought of sending it to someone but locked the screen instead.

[^23]: From Tao Yuanming's *"The Peach Blossom Spring."* Liu Ziji was the last person to seek the hidden utopia; he never found it. After him, no one asked the way again.
[^24]: refers to the Airbus A380 -- the world's largest passenger aircraft.
[^25]: Toulouse — a city in southwestern France and the site of a major Airbus final assembly plant.

*"To be honest, I've always believed my hometown is the best place in the world."*

Two weeks earlier I had reunited with a friend in Paris; for the first time I learned where he lived. When I mentioned my upcoming Côte d'Azur trip, he revealed his hometown lay right on the France-Italian border. He was Italian, yet carried none of the theatrical Italian accent; his hand gestures might even be fewer than mine. Last year, bored in class, he had been drilling Duolingo[^26] to get acquainted with Paris in advance. He failed some courses, yet his quirky ideas burst out of him like an American's across the Atlantic. I once told another Italian friend that "GPA cannot quantify a person's intelligence, execution, or talent"[^27] — he was the living proof. Where you grow up shapes who you become. Alfonso Cuarón's[^28] *Roma* ends with a plane roaring in the distance over Mexico's turmoil; someone who spent twenty years growing up on the Côte d'Azur may have drawn nourishment from the world through Nice airport. As for me, about to visit Monaco and a small Italian border town — beyond collecting stories for future dinners and nights out with friends, I was also doing it so that, two days later, the travel record on my DS-160[^29] would show up on every immigration officer's screen, and keep my U.S. visa from falling prey to Trump's[^30] latest One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

[^26]: Duolingo — a language-learning app.
[^27]: GPA — Grade Point Average, used to measure academic performance.
[^28]: Alfonso Cuarón — Mexican director, two-time Oscar winner for Best Director (*Gravity* and *Roma*).
[^29]: DS-160 — the U.S. online nonimmigrant visa application form (for tourist visas, student visas, etc.).
[^30]: Trump — Donald Trump, President of the United States.

Before my flip-flops could be worn through by waves and sand, I made it back to the host's place in time. He was busy on the phone while minding a child — his own or a friend's, I couldn't tell. I scrambled to pack, tossed still-damp winter clothes into the dryer for a quick spin, and just managed to gather my bags before eleven, ready to head for Monte Carlo.[^31] The host's call wasn't finished, but he came to the door anyway to shake my hand goodbye — parting felt exactly like meeting.

[^31]: Monte Carlo — Monaco's most famous district, renowned for its luxury casino, high-end hotels, and the Formula 1 Grand Prix.


Tao Yuanming had already imagined such an otherworld. The people of the Peach Blossom Spring — "they knew nothing of the Han dynasty, let alone the Wei or Jin"[^36] — embodied the earliest utopia.[^37] My first-year Chinese teacher in middle school loved to go off on tangents. He spoke of his old days in government work: first, two weeks to get through the final exams, then a whole month on *The Peach Blossom Spring* — riffing from the year 421[^38] all the way to the French Revolution.[^39] I never once heard him teach that text. My actual middle-school Chinese teacher, heavy with *banwei*[^40] rushed through the essay. All I remember: "Memorize the full text, spot check next week." The story sank like that, until I came to Leuven[^41] — the Eurostar[^42] shook me toward Paris at three hundred kilometers per hour, and then came the Louvre's[^43] free, all-encompassing embrace — the vertigo was overwhelming. Along the Grande Galerie,[^44] near-ten-meter canvases unfurled the French Revolution: from Napoleon's coronation[^45] to La Liberté guidant le peuple.[^46] Under Delacroix's[^47] brush, the goddess of Liberty strides barefoot over the barricade, hoisting the tricolor — the French from then on claimed freedom as their own; the Peach Blossom Spring still gathered dust in a middle-school textbook corner. Ten years later the realization hit: that Chinese class was my Enlightenment.[^48]

[^36]: From *The Peach Blossom Spring*: the villagers, having fled the wars of the Qin dynasty, were so isolated they had never heard of the Han, much less the Wei or Jin.
[^37]: Utopia — an imagined ideal and perfect society.
[^38]: The year 421 (Yongchu 2, Southern Song dynasty) — when Tao Yuanming composed *The Peach Blossom Spring.*
[^39]: The French Revolution (1789–1799) — the bourgeois revolution in France that overthrew absolute monarchy, rallying under the banner of "Liberty, Equality, Fraternity," widely regarded as foundational to the world's democratic systems.
[^40]: *Banwei* — Chinese internet slang for the weary, numb, rule-following aura office workers acquire from years of high-pressure work.
[^41]: Leuven (also spelled Louvain) — a city in the Dutch-speaking region of Belgium, about 20 km northeast of Brussels, home to KU Leuven.
[^42]: Eurostar — the high-speed cross-Channel train linking London with Paris and Brussels.
[^43]: The Louvre (*Musée du Louvre*) — in central Paris, originally a royal palace, now a museum; free for EU youth under 26.
[^44]: The Grande Galerie inside the Louvre, home to masterworks including *La Liberté guidant le peuple.*
[^45]: *The Coronation of Napoleon* (*Le Sacre de Napoléon*) by Jacques-Louis David, depicting Napoleon's 1804 self-coronation at Notre-Dame. The canvas is nearly ten meters long.
[^46]: *La Liberté guidant le peuple* (*Liberty Leading the People*), set against the July Revolution of 1830.
[^47]: Eugène Delacroix — 19th-century French Romantic painter, creator of *La Liberté guidant le peuple.*
[^48]: The Enlightenment — the 17th–18th-century European intellectual movement championing reason, liberty, and equality.

The next stop was Monte Carlo, and I had missed it, lost in memory. Google Maps[^49] flickered in and out as the train crossed through the Schengen Area[^50] and Monaco, briefly losing signal, and only after stepping out of Monaco's dim station — built inside a mountain cave[^51] — did I realize I had overshot. My halting French and an intermittent map signal led me to a village station. I pulled out my phone album. The location pin read: Carnolès.[^52]

[^49]: Google Maps — the most commonly used navigation app for overseas travel outside China.
[^50]: The Schengen Area — the zone of European countries that have abolished mutual border checks under the Schengen Agreement, allowing free movement. Monaco, though not a Schengen member, is entirely surrounded by France and maintains open borders.
[^51]: Gare de Monaco-Monte-Carlo — to conserve the principality's scarce land, the entire station is built underground in tunnels and rock caverns, with perpetually poor lighting.
[^52]: Carnolès — a **small** train station in southeastern France near the Monaco border.

The platform was empty. Just me, my oversized red Grand Frais shopping bag, and a ticket machine that may or may not take credit cards.

Four years ago, that platform wasn't empty. Hangzhou. A metro station I'd transferred through five hundred times. Every minute planned out, and still I missed the connection. She said she'd wait outside the convenience store at West Lake Cultural Square station. The store had a floor-to-ceiling glass wall. She leaned against it, head down, looking at her phone. From a distance I saw a purple silhouette, brown hair pinned with a black butterfly clip. Couldn't be sure it was her. I slowed my steps, walked to the glass door, and sent her a message telling her to turn around. Four months later she told me she'd spotted me long before that.

The platform across from Carnolès stood bare. Four years ago she saw me through the glass; I didn't see her. Now there's nothing on the other side, and I see it perfectly.

I first encountered this story's title in the subtitle of the first *Hobbit* film:[^34] Bilbo Baggins, content in Bag End, has his life upended by a wizard and dwarves, and sets off on an **unexpected journey**. Tolkien[^35] wrote a book out of it; mine fills only these three pages.

[^34]: The film's original title: *The Hobbit: An Unexpected Journey.*
[^35]: Tolkien — J. R. R. Tolkien, English author.

The westbound train came quickly. On the Côte d'Azur, trains run as frequently as Hangzhou Metro Line 5.[^55] When I was six, my father gave me a hardcover world atlas. Beneath the worn laminate was where my sense of the world began. Late at night, after lights-out, I taught myself to use a calculator, worked out GDP  per-capita for over two hundred countries, and reached a conclusion: the world's richest people live in Monaco. From that small room, I walked twenty years. I walked here.


[^55]: Hangzhou Metro Line 5 — a metro line running through several central districts of Hangzhou, with extremely high ridership and dense departures during rush hour.
